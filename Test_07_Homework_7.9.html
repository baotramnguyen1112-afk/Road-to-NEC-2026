A. Calling the contemporary world “urban” sounds straightforward until one asks what, precisely, is being counted. The label may refer to administrative municipalities, continuously built-up areas, or settlements that cross a threshold of population and density. National statistical offices have historically used different criteria, so a place classified as urban in one country could, under another country’s rules, remain rural. This is not a pedantic complication. If the categories themselves vary, cross-country comparisons can create an illusion of precision while combining unlike things. Recent international work therefore uses harmonised approaches such as the Degree of Urbanisation, which classifies settlements by population size and density rather than simply accepting each jurisdiction’s legal boundaries. Under this framework, the global distribution looks markedly urban: roughly four-fifths of people live in cities, towns or suburbs, while a smaller share live in rural areas. Yet even that aggregate conceals important distinctions. A megacity, a compact provincial town and a low-rise suburb may all fall on the non-rural side of a statistical divide while imposing very different demands on transport, housing and public services. The methodological lesson is consequently double-edged. Comparable definitions make global patterns easier to see, but they do not abolish the substantive diversity hidden inside the category “urban”. Any serious interpretation must keep the measurement rule in view rather than treating the resulting percentage as a self-explanatory fact.

B. The long-run transformation is nevertheless unmistakable. In the middle of the twentieth century, a much smaller proportion of humanity lived in settlements that meet today’s harmonised definition of cities. Since then, billions have moved, or been born, into increasingly dense settlements. The fastest growth has not occurred uniformly. Some very large cities in Asia and elsewhere expanded at extraordinary rates, while older metropolitan centres such as Tokyo and New York entered periods of slower growth after earlier expansion. This unevenness matters because “urbanisation” can describe at least two related but distinct processes: a rising share of a country’s population living in urban settlements, and the absolute growth of particular cities. A city may gain inhabitants while the national urban share changes only modestly; conversely, a country may urbanise rapidly through the proliferation of small and medium-sized towns rather than the explosive growth of one metropolis. Historical comparisons are further complicated by changing boundaries and improved satellite-based estimates. The dramatic global shift is real, but the trajectory is not a single procession toward ever-larger megacities. Much urban life occurs in smaller settlements, and some famous metropolitan giants are no longer among the fastest-growing. The most defensible account is therefore one of redistribution across a hierarchy of settlements, not an inevitable convergence on a universal urban form.

C. Why does the geography of settlement matter beyond demography? Density can place jobs, schools, clinics and consumers within easier reach, creating opportunities for specialisation and exchange. Urbanisation has often accompanied economic development, but correlation should not be mistaken for an automatic mechanism. A growing city can generate productivity gains when infrastructure and institutions allow people to connect efficiently; it can also magnify congestion, pollution, insecure housing and unequal access to services when population growth outruns provision. The same density that makes a bus network viable can make inadequate sanitation especially damaging. This is why averages comparing “urban” and “rural” living standards require care. Urban residents often have better access to electricity, water, healthcare and education, yet substantial deprivation persists inside cities, particularly in informal settlements. Moreover, an apparent urban advantage can mask large inequalities between neighbourhoods. Policy must therefore resist two symmetrical simplifications: romanticising rural life as inherently less precarious, and treating urban residence itself as a guarantee of prosperity. The relevant question is not merely whether people are concentrated, but whether institutions convert concentration into accessible public goods. Urbanisation changes the scale and geometry of provision; it does not by itself determine the quality of what is provided.

D. These measurement and policy problems converge in planning. Governments need population estimates to decide where to extend transport, schools, healthcare, water systems and housing, but the administrative map may fail to coincide with the functional city. Commuters can cross municipal borders daily; built-up areas can merge; peripheral settlements can become economically integrated long before legal boundaries are revised. A standardised spatial definition can reveal these continuities and make international comparison more coherent. It can also expose why official city totals sometimes disagree with globally harmonised datasets: the two may be measuring different geographic objects rather than one of them simply being wrong. Still, standardisation should be a tool, not a substitute for local knowledge. A global density threshold cannot tell planners which neighbourhood is vulnerable to flooding, where tenure is insecure, or whether a nominally accessible service is affordable. Nor can population projections settle political choices about whose mobility or housing should be prioritised. Better measurement narrows uncertainty, but it does not eliminate judgement. The practical value of urban data lies precisely in combining comparability with contextual interpretation: using common metrics to see large patterns while retaining enough local resolution to understand the lives and infrastructures those patterns compress.

PART 4 (Questions 88–100)

Read the following passage and do the tasks that follow.

1. Generative artificial intelligence has made an old educational temptation newly conspicuous: the temptation to confuse the production of an acceptable answer with the development of the capacities required to produce, evaluate and own that answer. A fluent system can compose a report, propose an argument or solve a routine problem in seconds. The educational question is therefore no longer adequately framed as whether students should “use AI”. Calculators, search engines and spellcheckers already made that binary obsolete. The more exacting question is what kinds of intellectual work may be delegated without hollowing out the judgement on which meaningful delegation itself depends. UNESCO’s recent work on AI and education repeatedly places human agency, critical judgement, creativity and ethical responsibility at the centre of this problem.

2. This emphasis is easy to misread as nostalgia for an analogue classroom. It is better understood as a distinction between assistance and substitution. Tools can enlarge the range of problems a learner can tackle, expose alternative explanations, or make feedback more immediate. Yet a learner who habitually accepts generated claims without interrogating their provenance, assumptions or omissions may become more efficient at completing tasks while becoming less competent at judging them. [I] The danger is not simply that an answer is wrong. A polished answer can be pedagogically more treacherous when its surface coherence discourages the very scrutiny that learning is supposed to cultivate.

3. The point becomes clearer if critical thinking is treated not as a detachable school subject but as a practice embedded in disciplinary activity. To challenge an AI-generated historical interpretation, a student needs some historical knowledge; to detect a dubious statistical inference, some grasp of evidence and probability is indispensable. General exhortations to “think critically” cannot compensate for an empty knowledge base. At the same time, knowledge that is never mobilised in comparison, explanation or evaluation can remain inert. The pedagogical task is thus reciprocal: build enough domain knowledge to make judgement possible, then create occasions on which judgement must actually be exercised.

4. Assessment is where this reciprocity becomes operational. If a task rewards only the final artefact, an AI system can obscure how much reasoning the learner supplied. Schools have consequently experimented with oral defence, supervised drafting, process logs, annotated prompts and comparison exercises in which students must diagnose weaknesses in generated material. None of these devices is foolproof, and surveillance can easily become disproportionate. Their stronger rationale is evidential rather than punitive: assessment should reveal the learner’s decisions, revisions and reasons instead of merely authenticating possession of a finished product.

5. Creativity poses a related difficulty. Generative systems can rapidly recombine stylistic patterns and offer variations that a learner might not have imagined unaided. That can be genuinely generative in the human sense when the output becomes material for selection, rejection and transformation. [II] But abundance can also flatten choice. When plausible options arrive before a student has struggled to frame the problem, the space in which an idiosyncratic intention might have formed can be pre-empted by statistically convenient suggestions. Creativity, on this account, is not protected by banning computational assistance; it is protected by preserving moments in which learners must originate purposes and criteria rather than merely curate options.

6. UNESCO’s competency frameworks accordingly describe students not simply as consumers of AI but as responsible users and co-creators. The formulation matters. “User” implies practical competence, including understanding what systems can and cannot reliably do. “Responsible” adds ethical and social considerations: bias, privacy, inclusion, environmental costs and the distribution of benefits. “Co-creator” goes further by insisting that human purposes remain visible in the interaction. [III] A student should be able to explain why a system was used, what contribution it made, what was rejected, and what standards governed the final decision.

7. Such ambitions also complicate equity. AI-supported tutoring or translation may widen access for learners who previously lacked specialised help. Conversely, uneven connectivity, language coverage, teacher preparation and institutional resources can reproduce or deepen existing inequalities. The relevant divide is therefore not captured by simple possession of a device. It concerns the quality of access: whether learners receive guidance that helps them question outputs, protect data and use systems for increasingly demanding work. [IV] An education system that distributes powerful tools without the surrounding intellectual and ethical infrastructure may democratise exposure while stratifying benefit.

8. The most durable response to AI in education is consequently neither prohibition nor technological surrender. It is curricular redesign around forms of human agency that become more, not less, important when machines can generate competent-looking language and analysis. Students need knowledge substantial enough to resist algorithmic authority, opportunities to make consequential choices, and assessments that make reasoning inspectable. The paradox is that the more capable AI becomes at supplying products of thought, the more deliberately education must cultivate the processes by which people decide what deserves to be thought, trusted, revised and acted upon.

The great libraries of Rome

Libraries have, through history, represented the ultimate repository of information and knowledge, and produced some of the most spectacular architecture in the Western world and beyond. Today’s libraries are a direct legacy of the Roman impulse to transcend practicality and invest arenas of knowledge with a sense of scale akin to that of churches – temples to a different creed – with their imposing porticoes and columns, their elaborate ornaments and staircases, their rows of desks and lofty shelves.

Yet a library cannot be reduced to the mere embodiment of universal education. The philosopher Michel Foucault once asserted that ‘power and knowledge directly imply one another’ – one cannot exist without the other. Where libraries have been designated as public, free and accessible to all, there has always existed the risk of echo chambers with curated contents and truths.

The institution of the public library goes further back than imperial Rome. During the earlier Roman Republic (509–27 BCE), libraries were a private affair, concealed in the properties of the educated elite and off-limits to the masses. They were overseen by a vir magnus (great man), the owner of the house, who would open his collection to his amici (friends), providing an intimate atmosphere for intellectual exchange and cultural influence and posturing.

In its later years, the Roman Republic was beset by a series of cataclysmic civil wars and political showdowns that sent it hurtling towards one-man rule. The Republic, with its imperfect checks and balances on political power, had failed; but to the Roman people, defined by their aversion to monarchism and the conviction of liberty, it was inviolable. The first emperor, Octavian, found himself navigating a careful balancing act of titles and epithets without ever assuming himself as Rex (king): from Princeps Civitatis (first citizen) to primus inter pares (first among equals), all with connotations of authority but not monarchical supremacy. He would settle on Augustus (the venerable one) as his official title. The end of the Roman Republic was never announced – despotism prevailed in the superficial likeness of the Republic.
